What Greeks tell you that position size doesn't

A derivatives risk monitor for a 12-position NSE options book — built to answer a question I kept running into at work: which position is actually carrying the most risk?

View code on GitHub Completed · May 2026

01The Problem

Options desks report risk primarily by notional and position count, because that's what's easiest to pull off a trade blotter. But notional doesn't tell you what a book actually loses money on. Risk managers, traders, and even risk committees routinely underestimate concentration because the position that "looks small" on a blotter can quietly carry half the book's directional risk — while a large position sits nearly flat because it's close to neutral Delta.

This isn't hypothetical. At Morgan Stanley, monitoring live options portfolios every day is exactly where this gap between notional and actual exposure shows up. Whoever owns the risk report — a risk manager, a desk head, a committee reviewing exposure limits — needs the answer in Greeks, not notional, or they're making decisions on the wrong number.

02My Thinking

The obvious first approach is to rank positions by notional and flag the largest ones for review — that's what most simple reporting tools do. I rejected that early: notional ignores option type, moneyness, and how positions net against each other. A second option was Delta alone, which is closer but still incomplete — Vega and Theta exposure can dominate a book's real risk profile even when Delta is small, particularly around events or near expiry.

I settled on a full Greeks engine — Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta, Rho — computed per position from first principles via Black-Scholes, aggregated at book level, paired with both historical and parametric VaR so the tail-risk answer didn't depend on a single distributional assumption.

Rejected

Rank by notional. Misses direction and option-type effects entirely — two positions with identical notional can have opposite portfolio impact.

Rejected

Delta only. Better than notional, but blind to Vega/Theta risk that can dominate an options book, especially around events or near expiry.

Chosen

Full Greeks engine + dual VaR. Black-Scholes Greeks per position, book-level aggregation, historical and parametric VaR side by side to expose tail-risk assumption error rather than hide it behind one number.

04Technical Stack

Python · pandas / NumPy

Vectorized Black-Scholes pricing across all 12 positions without per-row loops — the same approach scales cleanly to a book of hundreds of positions.

Black-Scholes-Merton

Closed-form pricing over Monte Carlo: faster, and NSE index/equity options are European-style with no early exercise to model. Closed-form Greeks are also directly differentiable, which matters for sensitivity accuracy.

matplotlib

A position-level heatmap is the fastest way to visually separate notional from Greek exposure. Static, archivable charts suit a risk report that gets shared and filed, not just viewed live.

Historical + Parametric VaR

Running both methods — rather than picking one — is itself the finding: the gap between them is exactly the kind of model risk a risk committee needs to see, not have hidden behind a single number.

05The input: a CSV with one row per position

The engine reads a single CSV file — sample_portfolio.csv — where each row is one options position. Ten columns describe everything the pricer needs:

ticker identifies the underlying. strike and spot are in the same currency (INR for NSE single-stock options, index points for NIFTY). T is time to expiry in years — 14 calendar days = 14/365 ≈ 0.038. sigma is the implied volatility as a decimal (0.14 = 14% IV), taken from the option's market price rather than historical realised vol. r is the risk-free rate (6.5% here, broadly in line with the RBI repo rate at the time). option_type is call or put. qty is number of lots — negative means short. lot_size is the NSE contract multiplier for that underlying: NIFTY = 50, Reliance = 250, HDFC = 550.

The full position size in units is qty × lot_size. A short 5 lots of NIFTY 24500 call (qty = −5, lot_size = 50) means −250 contracts. Every Greek gets scaled by that number, which is why a negative qty flips the sign of Delta, Gamma, and Vega — turning a long-option position into a short one with opposite risk characteristics.

06The position size view hides the real exposure

The heatmap below shows each position's Greeks scaled to actual portfolio exposure. The colour tells you where risk is concentrated — and it's not where the largest positions are.

The leftmost column shows each position's notional exposure (INR millions). RELIANCE 2800 PUT and HDFC 1700 Call have similarly large notionals — 7.1M and 7.6M. But look at the Delta column: RELIANCE 2800 PUT is red (negative delta — it's a long put, which loses when spot rises). HDFC 1700 Call is deep green (large positive delta). Same notional, completely opposite directional impact. The dashed line separates notional from the Greek sensitivities.
Net portfolio Greeks aggregated across all 12 positions. Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta — each tells a different story about what the portfolio is exposed to.

RELIANCE 2800 PUT and HDFC 1700 Call have essentially the same notional — about 7 crore each. But the RELIANCE position is a long put: it moves inversely with the underlying, contributing negative Delta to the portfolio. HDFC 1700 Call is a deep in-the-money long call with Delta close to 1 — almost every rupee of spot movement flows directly into the position. The result: HDFC 1700 dominates the portfolio's directional exposure while RELIANCE's large notional largely cancels itself between the offsetting put positions.

This is why options desks never talk about position size in isolation. The question is always how much Greek exposure a position carries — and that depends on the option type, moneyness, and how the position sits relative to everything else on the book.

07The cost of holding options that nobody talks about

The portfolio's net Theta is −3,131 per calendar day. That means the book loses that much in value just from the clock ticking — no market move required, no news event, nothing. Options are decaying assets, and Theta is what that decay looks like in rupees per day.

Most discussions of options risk focus on what happens when the spot price moves. Theta is the silent background cost that's running whether the market moves or not. Over a 30-day expiry cycle, this adds up to nearly INR 94,000 of time-value bleed — before you factor in any market moves. Understanding where this cost sits across the portfolio matters a lot when you're deciding which positions to hold through expiry.

08What a market crash looks like before it happens

The stress test below simulates 10 named scenarios — spot moves of ±5% and ±10%, volatility spikes, time decay, and combinations. NIFTY has seen −10% moves several times in the past five years. This is what the portfolio would have looked like going into one of those days.

Estimated P&L under 10 stress scenarios. The book is net long Delta — a crash hurts. But the positive Vega partially offsets it: volatility spikes in a crash, which benefits the portfolio.
Simulated daily P&L distribution. 95% VaR: −INR 107,200 (historical method) vs −INR 121,469 (parametric). The gap is what assuming normal returns costs you.

The stress test reveals something interesting: the worst-case scenario (−10% spot, +30% vol) is less bad than a naive reading of the Delta would suggest. The reason: this portfolio has positive Vega, meaning it benefits when implied volatility rises. A market crash typically comes with a spike in implied vol — and the Vega gain partially cushions the Delta loss. These two exposures are partially offsetting. Knowing that before the crash is the point.

09The gap between two ways of measuring risk

Historical VaR (−107,200) and parametric VaR (−121,469) give different answers for the same portfolio. The difference — about INR 14,000 — comes from an assumption: parametric VaR assumes daily returns follow a normal distribution. They don't. Real market returns have fat tails — extreme days happen more often than the normal distribution predicts.

The historical method doesn't make that assumption. It uses the actual distribution of simulated P&L. The parametric answer is faster to compute but consistently underestimates tail risk. This difference matters most precisely when you need VaR to be accurate — in volatile markets.

10Engineering Challenges

Sign conventions across a mixed book

Getting Delta, Gamma, and Vega to flip sign correctly for short positions (negative qty) while keeping Theta's sign convention consistent required careful, position-by-position testing. A sign error here silently reports a hedged book as directional, or the reverse — the worst kind of bug, because the output still looks entirely plausible.

Reconciling historical vs. parametric VaR

The two methods disagreeing by roughly INR 14,000 initially looked like a bug, until I confirmed it was the fat-tail effect the literature predicts. Validating that meant building a synthetic returns test against a known distribution to confirm the calculation was right before trusting the real result.

12Positions, 5 underlyings, one consolidated view

The most valuable part wasn't the code — it was constructing a multi-leg book and watching the Greeks interact. When you add a short put to a long call position, the Delta exposure shifts in ways that aren't always intuitive. Working with these numbers in a system built from first principles gave me a far more concrete mental model of how derivatives books actually behave — the Greek framework is the language that makes complex multi-position risk legible, and building the engine that produces those numbers made that language more concrete.

12Future Improvements

  • Live position feed instead of a static CSV snapshot, so the risk report refreshes intraday rather than on demand.
  • Multi-day P&L attribution — splitting realized P&L into Delta/Gamma/Vega/Theta contributions day over day, the same methodology used in my day job.
  • Correlation-aware stress scenarios instead of single-underlying parallel shocks, since independent shocks understate risk in a correlated market-wide crash.
