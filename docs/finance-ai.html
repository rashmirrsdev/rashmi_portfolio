Where quantitative risk management meets AI-assisted engineering — how I'm using agent-based AI tooling to rebuild the way risk gets visualised and deals get evaluated, not just calculated.

01Why This Section Exists

AI tooling has changed how fast a domain expert can turn what's in their head into working software. I spent three years learning exactly how commodity risk desks think — what a forward curve is actually telling you, why a time spread matters more than an outright price, where basis risk hides between related contracts. The gap was never the knowledge. It was the engineering time to turn that knowledge into a tool traders and risk managers would actually use.

That gap is closing. Working inside VS Code with GitHub Copilot and agent-based AI assistants, I can move from "here's how a risk manager thinks about this problem" to a working prototype in a fraction of the time it used to take — without outsourcing the judgment calls that actually matter. This section tracks two initiatives: what each one solves, how it's built, and what I'm learning about AI as a tool for financial engineering rather than a replacement for financial judgment.

AI workflow. GitHub Copilot and agent-based AI tooling in VS Code handle the data plumbing and scaffolding — ingestion, normalization, the repetitive parts of the visualization layer — while I own the judgment calls: which view a risk manager actually needs at 8am, what "correct" looks like for a given curve or spread calculation, and how the interface should behave when someone is checking it under time pressure, not browsing it at leisure.

UX principle. Built for a trader or risk manager glancing at it between meetings, not for someone with twenty minutes to explore a dense spreadsheet. Every view is designed to answer one question fast, with the option to drill in — not the reverse.

What it surfaces

Forward Curves

Interactive views of the full forward curve for each commodity — shape, shifts over time, and what's driving contango or backwardation on a given day.

Time Spreads

Calendar spread monitoring across contract months, surfacing roll risk and carry dynamics before they show up in P&L.

Cross-Curve Basis Spreads

Relative-value views across related curves — where two markets that usually move together start to diverge, and by how much.

Trade Baskets

Aggregated statistics and exposure views for baskets of related positions, so risk that's invisible trade-by-trade becomes visible at the basket level.

Decision support, not just reporting. The point isn't a prettier chart of the same numbers — it's a screen a risk manager can look at once and act on, instead of a report they have to interpret and then go verify against three other sources.

03Flagship: Deal Structure Analytics Dashboard

This is the initiative I'm most invested in right now. When a desk evaluates a new deal structure, the analysis today is fragmented by construction: risk analytics come from one tool, scenario P&L gets built in a spreadsheet, sensitivities come from a separate model, and someone — usually the most senior person in the room — has to reconcile all of it into a recommendation. Every handoff between tools is a place for a stale number or an inconsistent assumption to creep in, and the person approving the deal rarely sees the full picture in one place.

The Deal Structure Analytics Dashboard is a one-stop analytics platform built to let a risk manager evaluate a new deal structure — exposure, scenario P&L, risk metrics, and sensitivities — from a single interface, instead of stitching that picture together by hand every time a new structure comes up for approval.

This is not just analytics — it's decision support. The output isn't a set of numbers to interpret; it's an approval-ready comparison built to answer the question a Director or MD is actually asking: is this deal structure worth approving, and how does it compare to the alternative?

What it does

Deal Exposure

Understand the exposure a proposed deal structure creates before it's ever booked, not after.

Scenario P&L

Calculate P&L outcomes across multiple market scenarios in one pass, instead of rebuilding the spreadsheet for each new scenario.

Risk Metrics

The sensitivities and risk metrics a structure would carry, evaluated consistently rather than assessed one-off by whoever picks up the request.

Scenario Comparison

Compare multiple market scenarios side by side rather than one at a time — the comparison itself is often the insight.

Sensitivity Visualization

Visualize how a deal's value moves against each key input, so "what if rates move" has a direct visual answer, not a follow-up analysis request.

Approval Decision Support

A single, approval-ready view instead of five reconciled spreadsheets — built for the moment someone has to actually decide.

Why one interface matters

Bringing exposure, scenario P&L, risk metrics, and sensitivities into a single interface isn't a convenience feature — it removes an entire class of error. When four different tools each pull market data on their own schedule, it's possible for a scenario P&L figure and a sensitivities figure to be quietly built on different snapshots of the market. One interface means one source of truth for the numbers behind a single decision, and a faster path from "a new deal structure lands on the desk" to "we have an answer."

04How I'm Building Both

The workflow is agent-based AI development inside VS Code: GitHub Copilot and AI agents handle a large share of the implementation — scaffolding, boilerplate, iteration — while I own the parts that require actual domain judgment: what the tool needs to show, what "correct" looks like for a given risk or deal metric, and how a trader or risk manager should be able to interact with it under pressure, not just in a demo.

That division of labour is the actual point. AI tooling doesn't remove the need for someone who understands the market — it removes the friction between understanding the market and having something to show for it. Three years of daily Bloomberg monitoring across a live commodity book is what tells me which view actually matters to a risk manager at 8am; the AI tooling is what lets me build that view the same week instead of filing a request with an engineering team.

05The Bigger Goal

Risk and deal-evaluation tools at most desks look the way they've looked for a decade — dense tables, static reports, context split across five different tools. I want to change that: intuitive enough that a new analyst can read it in a minute, interactive enough that a trader can stress-test an idea live in a meeting, and robust enough that a Director or MD can rely on it in a risk review or a deal discussion. That's the standard I'm building toward, and AI-assisted development is what makes hitting it — as one person, alongside a full-time risk role — realistic.
