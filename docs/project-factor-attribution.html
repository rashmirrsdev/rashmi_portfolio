How much of a portfolio's return is actually skill?

Three years of Nifty 50 data. A 20-stock equal-weight portfolio. The question: how much came from picking the right stocks, and how much from just owning a rising market?

View code on GitHub Completed · April 2026
Jump to: 01 Problem 02 Thinking 03 Architecture 04 Stack 05 Results 06 Beta Shift 07 Diversification 08 Challenges 09 Impact 10 Future

01The Problem

Every fund manager, every analyst, every investor who's picked stocks believes they have some edge. The market went up, their portfolio went up more — so there must be skill involved. This isn't just an academic question — it's the exact question a CIO asks a portfolio manager at every performance review, and the exact question retail investors almost never ask themselves.

Return attribution — separating market-driven return from genuine stock-selection skill — is standard practice for institutional books; I do a version of it professionally. It's rarely done rigorously at the individual investor level or even by smaller funds that report gross returns with no decomposition. Without it, you cannot tell if a strategy is skill or luck — which means you can't decide whether to allocate more capital to it, replace the manager, or trust the track record going forward.

02My Thinking

The simplest approach — compare portfolio return to index return — tells you almost nothing. A portfolio that's just a leveraged version of the index would look like "skill" under naive relative performance. A single static CAPM regression over the full period is better, but it hides time variation in Beta, which turns out to be exactly where the real risk lives.

I chose a rolling-window OLS regression (60-day) decomposing returns into a market factor and an idiosyncratic residual, computed per stock and aggregated to the portfolio, paired with a concentration analysis (HHI, sector breakdown) as a second, independent lens on diversification.

Rejected

Naive relative performance (portfolio return vs. index return). Doesn't separate beta-driven co-movement from genuine stock-specific alpha.

Rejected

Static full-period CAPM Beta. Misses time variation entirely — hides exactly the regime-dependent risk that turned out to matter most during the 2022 drawdown.

Chosen

Rolling 60-day OLS + HHI/sector overlay. Captures how Beta shifts through time, and separates "diversified by position count" from "diversified by risk factor."

04Technical Stack

Python · pandas / NumPy

Vectorized rolling-window regression across 20 stocks and 3 years of daily data without row-by-row loops — the calculation has to re-run cleanly every time the window shifts.

statsmodels OLS

Transparent, auditable regression math over a black-box ML model. Attribution needs to be explainable to a portfolio committee, not just accurate — you have to be able to point at exactly why a number came out the way it did.

matplotlib

Static, report-ready charts, since the deliverable here is a research note, not a live-refreshing dashboard.

yfinance

Free historical price data suitable for a portfolio project without a Bloomberg terminal license — the same methodology carries over directly once real market data access is available.

0591 cents out of every rupee came from the index

The portfolio returned 30.97% over three years (Jan 2022 – Dec 2024). When you decompose that into market-driven and stock-specific components, the split is stark:

Total return: +30.97%
Market factor (beta-driven): +28.22%
Idiosyncratic (stock selection): +1.98%

91% of the return came from just owning the market.

Three years of daily returns decomposed. The orange line (market factor) tracks the portfolio almost exactly. The green line (idiosyncratic return) is the part that stock selection actually contributed — nearly flat by comparison.
Return, volatility, and Sharpe ratio by component. The market factor has a Sharpe of 0.71 — and you get that "for free" from any index fund. The idiosyncratic (stock-selection) Sharpe is 0.21.

The idiosyncratic Sharpe — 0.21 — tells you something uncomfortable: the stock-specific return wasn't very well compensated for the risk taken to get it. A passive index fund would have given you Sharpe 0.71 with no selection required. The 20-stock selection process added 1.98% of return over three years, while introducing stock-specific volatility that dragged the Sharpe down.

None of this means stock selection is pointless. But it does set a baseline: before claiming alpha, you need to strip out the market component first. Most "outperformance" claims don't do this carefully — they compare gross returns in a rising market without separating what the market handed to everyone.

06Beta isn't stable — and that matters in a crisis

The analysis uses rolling 60-day Beta estimates, meaning Beta is recalculated continuously as the market evolves. What the rolling Beta chart shows is that the relationship between individual stocks and the market isn't fixed — it shifts, sometimes substantially, and particularly during stress periods.

60-day rolling Beta for each of the 20 stocks over 3 years. Notice the convergence and shift during the 2022 drawdown period — the betas you measured in a calm market weren't the betas you got during the fall.
Beta by stock, by time period. Darker = higher market sensitivity. Financial stocks show more volatile beta estimates over time; FMCG stocks stay more stable — a real market behaviour pattern.

This matters because portfolio hedging decisions are made based on estimated Beta. If you bought a hedge based on June's Beta and the crisis comes in October, the Beta has already shifted. In the 2022 drawdown period, correlations across stocks spiked and Betas moved — the portfolio behaved more like a concentrated market bet than the diversified holdings it appeared to be on paper.

This is one of the well-documented but underappreciated dynamics in equities: in a crash, everything becomes correlated. The diversification benefit shrinks precisely when you need it most.

07The diversification you think you have vs. the diversification you actually have

By any standard measure, the equal-weight 20-stock portfolio is well-diversified. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) — a standard concentration metric — comes out at 0.05, which corresponds to the equivalent of 20 perfectly equal positions. Nothing is outsized.

Distribution of daily idiosyncratic (alpha) returns — centred near zero, with thin tails. This confirms that the portfolio's risk is overwhelmingly systematic, not position-specific. The diversification is working as measured.

But that diversification is measured by position count. The sector breakdown tells a different story: 30% of the portfolio is in Financials (6 stocks). In a banking sector shock, those 6 stocks won't diversify each other much — they'll move together because they're driven by the same underlying factors (rate decisions, credit cycles, regulatory news). The apparent position-level diversification hides a meaningful sector concentration.

This is the difference between diversification by count and diversification by risk factor. A portfolio can look perfectly spread on paper while being highly concentrated in a single economic driver. The only way to see this is to decompose the exposure — which is exactly what attribution frameworks are built to do.

08Engineering Challenges

Rolling Beta window selection

Short windows (20-day) produced noisy, unstable Beta estimates; long windows (120-day) were too slow to capture the actual 2022 drawdown shift. I tested multiple window lengths against that known crisis period and settled on 60 days as the practical middle ground — responsive enough to matter, stable enough to trust.

Diversification metrics that disagree

Position-level HHI alone said the portfolio was well-diversified (0.05, equivalent to 20 equal positions). It took a separate sector-level decomposition to catch the 30% Financials concentration that the position-count metric was structurally blind to — one metric wasn't wrong, it just wasn't sufficient on its own.

The result I expected going in was that most return would be market-driven. The result I didn't fully expect was how clearly the data shows the gap between measured diversification and experienced diversification during stress. The honest implication: for anyone managing a portfolio of listed equities, the first question before evaluating performance should be "what did the market contribute?" If you can't answer that, you can't say anything meaningful about whether you've added value.

10Future Improvements

  • Multi-factor model — extend from single-factor market Beta to a Fama-French-style model (size, value, momentum), so idiosyncratic return is measured against a richer benchmark than just the index.
  • Multi-asset attribution — extend beyond equities to commodity and derivatives factor exposures, drawing directly on my day-to-day work.
  • Real-time recalculation instead of a static historical report, so the rolling Beta and attribution update as new data arrives.
